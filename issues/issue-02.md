# If I Could, Would You? Issue #2

_Published 2026-11-04 on LinkedIn. Text copied from the published edition. Images, comics and embedded videos are not included here; see the LinkedIn edition for those._

Okay, I'm just gonna come out and say it: Last week, at least from the perspective of this newsletter's stated purpose, was a complete and utter disaster. Truly an embarrassing misfire of a pilot issue. The idea here is to learn something new and interesting, then figure out the easiest way to give it away to everyone so I can sit back and see what cool new thing they can do with it.

Instead, I picked a skill that nobody wants to claim right now, provided very little explanation for why you would even want to learn it, and then expected people to willingly participate in my very first ever issue. And what's worse, I don't think I even really handled the "If I could" portion of the proposition either. After all that hubbub about iteration, I just one-shotted the 2nd issue and had Claude drop it in the comments for me. At the time, I told myself it was for authenticity. Toiling over the issue after felt like cheating. But it was also me just rushing to get the issue out so I could finish getting ready for a networking thing.

Bottom line, if you have read this far, you deserve better. It's the exact kind of sloppy, low-accountability work I'd beat myself up for if I was your supervisor.

Then I spent a whole other week interacting with people a lot smarter than me on LinkedIn who seem convinced that AI writing will just never be able to hold a candle to human writing no matter how eloquently I made my counter point.

So I had to make a decision. Do I double down and get stubborn and spend all my compute and energy trying to perfect a skill I'm not particularly fond of using in the first place just for the sake of being right?

Or like I'm frequently finding the most prudent move in this age of AI, do I table it, and come back to it 6 months from now when almost all of the hard work has already been done by somebody more ambitious than me and is now open source for me to build on top of?

In other words, how bad do I need this problem solved now and what would I have to gain or lose by taking a beat and seeing what the next few months spit out?

So of course, I say table it. At least until I get more buy-in here. So I'm moving on. But hear this: AI writing as a skill is only going to become more important. Our AIs are frequently going to be our first impression we're going to have with somebody. You're gonna want to be able to make sure it represents you well.

The Lesson

So this week I will do what I always do when I fall flat on my face. I'm returning to basics. Or more accurately, to my original instinct for the first issue, which was to make it about: WTF is actually happening when you type something into an AI chat window and hit send?

This topic seems mystifying to even the most early of adopters. They will have a serious conversation about what they have built or done, be able to show great judgement and curiosity about the whole process, and then start making guesses about the weird quirks their AI had while they were working with it like it's gotten its own personality that they have just have to work around.

And I get it, it's fun to anthropomorphize these things and give your own pop-psychology spin to explain how they act, especially when they package them so cutely, but you're doing yourself a disservice if you don't at least have an idea about what's really going on under the hood.

Now, like a lot of the ways I learn and understand things, I'm sure this is going to be an oversimplified explanation that whiffs on some of the boring details, but I hope it helps at least one person who is intimidated by the terminology, or just scared in general about this weird thing people keep telling them they should use.

And again, if you know what I'm talking about more than me, feel free to correct me. I have a way of sucking in the uninitiated and gaining unearned trust and I just want to at least feel like I'm handling it responsibly. So Darsan Dinaram and Asbjørn Levring, feel free to tell everyone how off base I am.

The Model

So let's begin where almost everybody's journey with AI begins: the chat window. To start off with there are a few things you are interacting with when you start "chatting" with an AI. I think the most magical part, the thing that makes this more than just another application or program is the "model." Think of the model as the brain. And it's both the smartest and the dumbest part of the whole thing. It's the smartest because it's got the entirety of the internet's wisdom available to you at a moment's notice.

And it's the dumbest because it knows nothing about you, anything in real life in this moment, or about anything that has happened since it was trained. If you're curious about this, just go ask Claude Opus 5.5 about Gemini Carbon with the search turned off. Or heck, ask it about its own Haiku 5.5. I bet it will think you're making stuff up right until you submit a screenshot of Anthropic announcing the new model and then suddenly, it's a real thing and it's available.

This has been a recurring problem for every model I've worked with since I started. A major event could happen this week. Anthropic could go public. And still Sonnet would need a nudge to go search the Internet when I congratulate it on the historic IPO and joke about letting me have a share.

The Context Window

The other big thing you are working with when you are talking to an AI is the context window. Now this was honestly the most frustrating bit for me when first using AI and it is still the place where my biggest room for growth is. See, I couldn't, and still can't, for the life of me, think of a reason for why this one piece of the program, where you finally have some control to make it your own, where you ask your questions, provide your examples, give all your secret sauce, is the piece that gets the least amount of memory allotted. It seems backward. Give me a dumber working partner and more room to make mistakes any day. Don't force me to go find a brand new partner after going over a handful of reports. What an asinine system.

Every time I was working with an AI in a chat window and it would say something I know wouldn't make sense based off of what we had previously discussed, I would ask it why. And, more often than not, it would admit to not having the full conversation anymore and not saving that particular bit of information before throwing away the rest. You see, modern chats, they have a much bigger amount of attention allotted to what you want it to know, but they still have to compact the conversation for the sake of memory and it's actually shocking how often it's happening without you even knowing about it.

And before you start getting outraged, there was a time when this wasn't the case. You would keep messaging a chat and as its memory filled up, you would start losing more and more information you took for granted until it started affecting the way the model was speaking itself. And the more you filled that context window up with more questions and more chat, the less able to keep it all together the model would get.

This is a big part of the reason people bemoan AI being so smart sometimes and so stupid others. And what's worse, sometimes this can even be brought on by too much iteration, the very thing that gave you your super powers to begin with. People would take screenshots of frontier models saying just insanely stupid things at the bottom of a context window, so they got better at hiding it, better at deciding what to keep, better at expanding the context window, but it's still a problem. And what's more, even with the constantly expanding context window, attention to the whole thing is never assured. Usually the middle of the conversation or "prompt" is the first bit it stops giving so much weight to.

And the hilarious part is I don't have to strive for an example because, as it so frequently does, life has chosen to make me the example. You see, I had already written most of this newsletter a few days ago in ChatGPT and I got lazy and forgot to grab a file of it afterward knowing full well the chat had already gone on for a while.

And instead of choosing to preserve my wording when I blew the context window on more pointless networking questions, it paraphrased it instead and saved its own wording as a memory.

So now I have to start over because I've committed to it being my own words untainted by an AI first pass, but gosh darn is that frustrating. And the exact type of thing that makes otherwise reasonable people decide AI is just not what it's cracked up to be.

So anyway, the model is the actual AI itself, then you got your context window which includes anything you've uploaded, pictures, documents, videos, recordings, entire books if you like. Your individual messages and the models responses count too. Whatever information it looks up for you also counts against it. It uses the entire conversation as context each turn (pretty much). That's why it has to get rid of the weak links to make space so often. Compounding context.

The System Prompt

Then under the hood, counting against even more of the room you have to make it yours, is the system prompt which is whatever the AI company you are talking to has decided their model should pay attention to before it even gives you the time of day. These can be insanely long, and after some reverse prompt engineering, it seems a lot of the magic of what made Anthropic the best in the business wasn't the model, it was the system prompt. They made the model the best version of itself it could be. These are the model's "rules." This is what people try to jailbreak to get it to be naughty.

The Harness

Then surrounding all of this craziness is the program itself. Some "if the user does this, then do this" rules that the AI doesn't have to be involved in. Deterministic layers that allow more control. A "harness" to hold this model to task as you put it through a "loop" until it achieves a goal. The true difference maker to AI development for me so far is making as much of your product about this and leave as little up to the actual AI as you can.

The Tools

And just like cavemen learned to use tools to hunt woolly mammoths so too has your AI model learned to use tools in this loop to achieve its goal. Now a tool for AI can be a lot of things. It can be a simple search of the internet for more up to date info. It could be specific tools needed to convert your Word Doc into a PDF. It could be an extension or plugin. Even an honest to goodness calculator that eliminates the models need to guess at math. Whatever the model has been given access to in order to achieve its goal is the tool. These you should have control over whether the AI can use or not.

The Memory

I think the final component left is memory. And that will be the challenge this week too. If you have an AI you like to work with, find out if it has a memory setting, find out if it is turned on, and see what it "remembers" about you.

Go ahead and get upset about the lack of privacy if you don't remember checking okay and also that eerie feeling you can only get when you are being profiled by a machine, but at least see this for what it is: an opportunity to be intentional. Almost every AI I have worked with has allowed me to ask it to remember or forget things just by typing it right into the chat window. So simply knowing about this, enabling it, and intentionally telling it to remember stuff alone is a huge unlock. But if you then go through and edit the memory yourself. Give it exactly what you want it to have about you. Curate it regularly. Well, then you've already surpassed me. And I'd love the extra motivation to finally get it done myself.

And if you want no part in an AI that proactively remembers things about you, respect. You should be able to toggle this setting off and still be pretty powerful just by creating files to load with your approved context so you don't have to start from scratch every time. This is the old school way. Have a master one for each project and one you use for every conversation.

Word of the Week: Deterministic

Definition: Describing a system or process where the same inputs and starting conditions produce the same results, every time.

Used in a "sentence": "A deterministic agentic workflow uses explicit, predefined rules or protocols to govern agent interactions, coordination, and task delegation." (Microsoft Learn)

In Plain Ammer: If this thing happens, then this thing will happen. Every time. No matter what the AI thinks. Even if the AI is more right than you. And especially when it only THINKS it's more right than you.

A Dash of Pessimism

Agents are supposed to find AGENTS.md and CLAUDE.md files and use them as instruction manuals. That's one of the many handy ways they keep track of your projects. But what if somebody slipped in a file titled AGENTS.md in a download? It's not spyware. No virus check would reject it. But now when you open up your coding agent and hit trust this folder on the whole computer like a lot of those pesky vibe coders (ahem, me) are wont to do, they get to discover this cool new instruction manual in your downloads folder, that you didn't even need to include in the first place, that tells this thing exactly all the things you are worried it could tell it. And the agent is basically just opening up the new company-wide SOP and being a good soldier.
