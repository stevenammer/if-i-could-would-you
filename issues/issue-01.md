# If I Could, Would You?

_Published 2026-10-28 on LinkedIn. Text copied from the published edition. Images, comics and embedded videos are not included here; see the LinkedIn edition for those._

where I find out what I can do, then find out if I can teach it away in a way that inspires others.

Welcome to the first edition of If I Could, Would You? I'm glad you're here. This week I wonder if I can truly outsource the thing that is most me: my writing. And then figure out whether any of you would want to learn how in the first place. But before I expect you to read a wall of text in the age of AI, I suppose I should present my credentials:

I have none.

I'm not in IT, my degree is an associates in general studies that took me 10 years to complete, and I had virtually no experience with AI until about 8 months ago when I started my job hunt again and every site I uploaded my resume to helpfully offered to punch it up with an AI so it won't be screened out by... their other AI... For a Fee. Which is a special kind of evil when your entire purpose for being there is, presumably, needing an income in the first place.

My biggest flex tech wise up until that point was writing the entire final group project for my C Programming class which, frankly, IS pretty impressive because, until then, I had always ended up with the job of "presenter" when it came time to hand out jobs during group projects. But this one was different. I wrote the entire thing in Notepad on my work computer, with no compiler, almost compulsively, while slinging vacation packages outbound at night. I'm still proud of it.

But that was 15 years ago, I haven't written a line of code since, and it was a word search generator.

It's also eerily similar to how I "vibe" coded a reading app for my daughter that scans the cover of any book, grabs its legal digital copy online (if we have access to it), assigns it a reading level, creates a read along version of it that keeps with her pace so she doesn't have to keep her finger on the screen, creates a test based only off the materials in the book, incorporating what she's learning in her grade level this year, then grades her answers and gives her "coins" she can use for new dumplings in her custom fidget trading game and new outfits in her roblox-free dress to impress game I vibe coded her the week before.

So this is not a technical master class. It's a passion project. I fully expect to get things wrong and have to go back and fix it later. I'm learning in public. That being said, if you are an expert and see me steering people down a wrong path, please correct me. I'm heading that way too at a break-necking pace.

Now that we got all that icky boasting out of the way, let's move to our lesson of the week: writing with AI. I don't know if it's because I got rage baited and left a comment on the wrong post or if my LinkedIn feed really represents reality, but it seems a lot of people are concerned about AI writing.

Everyone now seems to be some kind of literary expert: confidently claiming to be able to spot AI "slop" a mile away as if nobody could ever get one past them. And I gotta say I've never been so confident in something in my whole life as these folks are when they describe themselves not being duped by something that has enabled paralyzed people to not only miraculously move again, but also to actually "feel".

Do you really think if they put that same compute towards making the text output fool your eyes, they would somehow lose? Or how many people are you going to disrespect by questioning their authenticity in your pursuit of proving how un-gettable you are? It's the exact type of unearned confidence that makes my blood boil.

So let's fool them. I think the central problem with writing with AI today is people are almost completely focused on either how to make AI sound less like a bot or how to make it sound more like a human when what they should really be concerned with is how to make it sound more like them. That is, assuming they're a human.

So I challenge you. Instead of giving your AI a list of rules somebody else told you it should follow to sound like a person, why don't you give it things you've actually written? Don't write a lot? Give it access to your (sanitized) emails or text messages. Give it something you said out loud. Have it go through your previous conversations if it has a memory of them. Hit the little mic button and just talk to it about what's on your mind. Whatever way you can find to get your authentic talking and thinking into your AI.

Then ask it to figure out how to write like you. Have it ask you questions about what your intended meaning was, why you chose certain wording, why you thought certain things. It's honestly a fascinating process.

Then be the terrible coworker that points out every single thing that's wrong. Tear it apart. Tell it what you think is stupid. Keep doing it until you like what you see. All of those rules become a write like you prompt or skill or however you want to use that gets better every time you correct it.

If you do that, I think you'll be pleasantly surprised with the results. And if you want to skip all the work, copy and paste the prompt on this page into your AI and it should guide you through the rest.

A Dash of Pessimism

One of the beautiful things about being broke and time constrained, is it forces you to absolutely maximize your bang for your buck. It makes you come up with creative solutions to problems other people don't even think about. One of the most obvious cost cutting solutions, whether you are an experimenter like myself or a big company trying to cut costs and protect your information, is hosting open source models locally. Frankly, it's become pretty easy to do, only costs what you spend to power it, and these days, you can run a model that does what frontier models did 6 months ago on a laptop.

It's like being a tool guy in a free tool store that comes out with cooler tools every day.
