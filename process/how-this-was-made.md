# How this newsletter gets made

I lost a whole draft to a chat window's memory, and the AI "helpfully" saved its own version of my words instead. That's where this starts.

## Read this first

I want to show you how "If I Could, Would You?" actually gets made, including the parts where I looked bad.

One honest caveat up front. Most of what follows comes from one long record that ChatGPT assembled for me on November 9. It pulls together my writing, my prompts and my notes from late October through early November. The original chat exports were not recovered. So the quotes below come from that AI-assembled record, not from verbatim transcripts. Where the record itself says a quote is a partial fragment or a recollection, I'll tell you. I'd rather give you a labeled, imperfect trail than a tidy story where every decision looks obvious.

## The short version

I write the issues. AI helps me find things, check things, argue with me and keep track of drafts. It gets things wrong constantly. My job is catching that, and sometimes catching myself.

Here's the arc.

## Issue 1: an irritation with a title on it

Issue 1 started with a title and an annoyance. I'd picked "If I Could, Would You?" over an earlier candidate, "Allow Me to Translate." The promise was simple: I find out what I can do, then find out if I can teach it away.

The subject was writing with AI. I'd been watching people confidently claim they could spot AI writing from a mile away, and I wanted to test the other side of it. My thesis was that everyone chases "less like a bot" or "more like a human" when the real target is "more like you."

I rewrote it a lot. I tried a few ways of poking at the self-proclaimed experts before landing on "some kind of literary expert." I added a joke about a coworker bonking you over the head, then took it out. "Try. Again." became "Try again." Smaller and flatter won.

I also swapped out science stories that didn't hold up. One was a cartilage story that turned out to be older and less proven than it looked. One was a boast about turning mercury into gold, which I dropped once it was clear nobody could back up the claim. The version that stayed was a personalized vaccine story, and I corrected one word in it, "picture" to "piece," because the word changed what the sentence said.

The record also shows I made the AI write the Word of the Week example out of a real source, not an invented one. More on that below.

## The miss

Issue 1 was, by my own measure, a miss. I said so at the top of Issue 2, and I still think that's right. I picked a skill hardly anyone wanted to claim. I didn't explain why you'd want it. I asked for participation before I'd earned any. And after all my talk about iteration, I rushed the follow-up. The published Issue 2 says part of the reason was that I wanted to get on with something else that night. That's not a process, that's a shortcut.

## Issue 2: three drafts, one lost

Issue 2 was not "draft one, then draft two." It was several different articles.

An AI first draft came first. It was about running my own writing workshop on myself. It was labeled as AI and unreviewed, and I didn't use it. It was a different idea from the one I ended up writing. The AI also wrote notes on its own draft, listing the sentences least likely to sound like me and the sourcing gaps. That was useful. It still wasn't mine.

Then I changed the subject entirely. I went back to my original instinct for Issue 1: what actually happens when you type something into an AI chat window and hit send? That one I wanted to write myself.

Then I lost the draft. I'd written most of it inside a long ChatGPT chat. The chat ran long, the context got compacted, and the assistant paraphrased my wording and saved its own version as a memory. I'd forgotten to save a copy. I committed to starting over in my own words, with no AI first pass.

The record shows that the earliest version is gone for good. All that survives are fragments and later rewrites. I'm not going to reconstruct the lost version and pretend it's the real thing.

The rewrite went through several complete versions on November 4. Some edits were small: "embarrassing" and "pilot issue" moved around, and a swear word got softened. One was bigger. An early draft called AI our future "spokespersons," and the published version says AIs are often our "first impression." That's a different idea, not a polish.

A satire I wrote also existed alongside this. I cut its original framing before publication. An AI-voiced adaptation of it is linked in the issue, clearly labeled as fiction.

## Twelve times I steered, and what happened

These are the moments where I rejected or redirected the AI. The quotes are mine, as they appear in the AI-assembled record. A few were lightly trimmed to leave out profanity. Dates come from the record, not from original chat logs.

**1. Don't touch my words.**
On November 2 I wanted to see a piece of my writing next to the assistant's edits. I told it: "Let's see it all together in my words don't muck it up show me the stitches in red."
I wanted the AI's changes visible and separate, never blended in. That became a rule: my words in one place, AI proposals in another.

**2. Did you change anything?**
Minutes later I asked: "Did you edit my wording at all?" Then: "Then give it untouched."
I needed to know. If the AI had quietly changed things, I couldn't trust anything it handed back.

**3. The one that stayed.**
For the optimism section of Issue 2, I turned down a run of stories about young inventors: teenage prodigies, kids making games, a child with a nature-tracking app. The record says the one that won was a group of fifth graders who built a Braille tool and talked to a real accessibility expert. My reaction: "Yeah the braile is the one."
I wanted kids, a real result and something a reader could check. One honest note: the published Issue 2 ended up with a shorter, more general paragraph about child inventors, and the Braille story isn't in it as a full write-up.

**4. Keep the AI out of the hard part.**
This is for the next issue. After the assistant ranked my ideas, I wrote: "Once you know what the AI is... And what a loop is, everything else really is just a rule or a wrapper."
That is the core of what I'm working on for the next issue.

**5. Weak objections.**
The assistant kept saying my loop example was an oversimplification. I pushed back: "Earlier you said it was an oversimplification AI says that a lot and the way it breaks my example is usually never really strong."
I wasn't going to water the idea down because the assistant reached for a stock objection. If it can break my example, it should do it with something strong.

**6. The rest is capabilities.**
A bit later I tightened my own point: "I think the point I'm making is that is the gist of it and the rest is just increasing capabilities given or created as you iterate."
That's how I think about it. The loop is the core, and the extras are things you add as you go.

**7. Not fake, not funny.**
When we hunted for a video, I got blunt with the shortlist: "4 is garbage. 3. Is the leader. 1. Is hackneyed and fake."
I didn't want a staged skit pretending to be an AI doing something. I wanted something real.

**8. Real life, and shorter.**
On the same hunt, I said: "I like that, but I want something in real life and shorter.. actual robotics?"
The favorite at that point was long and simulated. I wanted real hardware and a short clip.

**9. Don't trust one model.**
After a round of video candidates, I wrote: "Keep looking and give me a prompt to look in other llms."
I'd rather check one AI against another than accept a single answer.

**10. You're using the old file.**
The assistant came back with an older handoff that listed some choices as still open. They weren't. I wrote: "That ain't right I decided on optimism and Pessimism, find the chat."
It had missed decisions I'd made later that evening. More on that in the next section.

**11. Show me the bones.**
When I asked for the plan for an issue, I didn't ask for a draft. I wrote: "Okay, man show me the bones of the whole issue and I will start writing."
I'll take structure from an AI. The sentences are mine.

**12. A rule about what to learn from.**
The record attributes one more line to me. It comes from an AI draft that quoted me, not from a chat I can show you, so treat it as secondhand: "Be careful not to pull signal from ai written stuff especially in my drive."
Even if the exact wording is off, the idea is: don't teach an AI my voice from text an AI wrote.

## Where the AI got it wrong, and how I caught it

I'd rather list these plainly than pretend they didn't happen.

- **It replaced my words with its own.** The paraphrased, lost draft above is the big one. A coding-tool editing session also reportedly softened my language, dropped a swear word and missed a reference without looking anything up first. I caught it because the result didn't sound like me.
- **It used a stale handoff.** The file it relied on predated my last decisions. I knew because I'd made them.
- **It made up a quote.** For the Word of the Week, an assistant wrote a jargon sentence that sounded corporate and real. The record says so plainly: "It was made up by an assistant." I asked for a real sourced one, and the published issue uses a quote from Microsoft's documentation.
- **It served stale or weak science.** The cartilage story looked new and wasn't. One biomedical story got credited to AI with no evidence AI was essential. A vaccine trial that lowered recurrence risk could easily be written up like a general cure. In the cartilage case, I dropped it after finding the original report was older and showed no treatment in living humans.
- **It overstated a security test.** An early description of a test was wrong. After I saw the actual commands, I rewrote the sentence to say only what happened. The record's checklist says to report that an outbound test was attempted and blocked, not that data was stolen.
- **It inflated my background.** The record says an AI draft gave me a bigger accomplishment than I'd earned and that I rejected it as inaccurate. That one is secondhand, from the AI draft's own account.
- **It offered fake comedy.** Its video shortlist included a staged skit, which I called out in moment 7.

The pattern: the AI is smooth and fast and confident, and it's often wrong in ways that sound right. Checking is the job.

## What AI did, and what it didn't

**What it did.** It helped me find candidate stories and sources. It checked claims, sometimes badly. It argued with me. It kept notes. It drafted things I threw out. It turned my scattered notes into the record this document is based on.

**What it didn't do.** It didn't write my sentences. My rule is that my words and the AI's words stay separate, labeled and approved. It didn't decide what the issue is about. It didn't know when a joke was working. It couldn't tell me which of my ideas was too long. And it didn't read my mind: the record shows how many times I had to say "that's not what I meant."

I should also say what I can't show you. The record doesn't hold every message, every assistant reply or every version of the comics. Some of my lost writing is just gone.

## What my AI thinks I should do differently

I asked the AI agent that helped put this together what it thinks I should change. Claude wrote this section, not me. Every point comes from something in the record above, and I haven't decided which ones I'll follow.

- **Save your own words before a long chat.** The lost Issue 2 draft is the clearest failure in the record. Copy what you've written into a plain file first, so no assistant can paraphrase it into a memory and call that the original.
- **Don't let the calendar set the deadline.** Your own published words say you rushed Issue 2 out so you could get ready for a networking event. A rushed issue after a miss is how a miss becomes a pattern.
- **Say why before you ask.** Issue 1 asked readers to take part in something it hadn't given them a reason to want. Start the next one with the payoff.
- **Give the AI smaller jobs.** It was most useful finding candidates, checking links and arguing with you. It was least reliable when it wrote full paragraphs, quoted from memory or leaned on an old handoff. Ask it for three options, not the paragraph.
- **Keep one current decisions file, and make it the first thing the AI reads.** The stale handoff that listed settled choices as open cost you time. A single file you update the moment you decide something fixes that.
- **Keep the trail as you go.** This archive had to be rebuilt after the fact, and some of it couldn't be. Next time, log the steering moments while they happen.

If you want to try this yourself, start with the first one. Keep your own words somewhere the AI can't rewrite them. Then see what it does with them.
