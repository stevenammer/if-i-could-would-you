If I Could, Would You?

where I find out what I can do, then find out if I can teach it away in a way that inspires others.

Welcome to the second edition of If I Could, Would You? Last week I handed you a prompt and told you to teach your AI how to write like you, so this week I ran the thing on myself. Mostly because you can't really assign homework you haven't done.

The first thing my AI nearly learned was how to sound like another AI.

Let me back up. Step one of my own prompt is to feed it things you've actually written, and if you don't write much, I told you to hand it your emails or your texts. So my AI went through mine looking for something casual I'd typed myself and came back with... nothing it could use. Everything I'd sent lately was automated or job hunt boilerplate, and my texts live on my phone, where it can't see them.

So it moved on to my Google Drive and ran straight into files that other AIs had written about me. Which, in fairness to it, were technically about me. They just weren't BY me. One of the files it ended up throwing out had a footer that said, right there at the bottom, it was built in Claude.

I caught it and typed this, lowercase ai and all:

"Be careful not to pull signal from ai written stuff especially in my drive."

To its credit, it listened. It threw out everything it couldn't prove I typed, and here's what survived: five Word documents from 2017 and 2018, with my name sitting in the file's author field, and the tip text from the weekly sales newsletters I used to write. That's the whole pile. That's every word it could actually prove was me, and all of it was written before any of this AI stuff existed.

It gets worse, or better, depending on whether you're me. There was also an older voice guide on my computer, written by an AI, that described me as someone who "built a 40-agent enablement function from scratch."

It's a beautiful sentence. I'd hire that guy.

What actually happened is I supervised the late shift on an inbound floor of about 40 agents at a vacation sales call center, and I wrote training materials nobody asked me to write. I never built a function. I didn't build anything from scratch except a stack of Word documents, which, as we've now established, are the only verified proof I exist.

My AI wrote me a better resume than I have.

I'm not using it. My rule is pretty simple, I don't claim more than what happened, even when the robot phrases it that nicely.

Then came the questions, which is the part of the process I called fascinating last week. It wanted to know why "I have none." got its own line in the first issue. That one I could actually answer:

"I was trying to give it room for the laugh. Executives used to a long list of qualifiers before people speak."

Then it asked what I'd cut to get the line that short. My full, considered response:

"I dunno. Nothing I think."

That's in my voice file now, as an actual rule. Short lines don't get padded, because nothing was trimmed from them to begin with. My shrug got written up as policy.

So here's my one real piece of advice for this week. Before you hand your AI "your writing," ask a dumber question first: who typed this? Your last cover letter, the one the job site offered to fix? The meeting recap? The LinkedIn post you had it "punch up" right before you hit publish, the thank you email, the bio, the out of office message you were too tired to write yourself at 6pm on a Friday... If an AI touched it, your AI is going to learn that AI, it isn't learning you, it's learning your ghostwriter. And do you actually know which of your own files are yours?

Same week, different robot. I've got an automated tracker watching my job hunt, and it told me nothing new had come in. Meanwhile there were [number redacted in this public copy] rejection emails sitting in my inbox. It only looked back three days...

I found them the old fashioned way, by going through my inbox by hand. Since I promised to do this in public, the running total is [job-hunt figures redacted in this public copy].

So that's my AI this week. One version of it wanted me to sound more impressive than I am, and another one didn't want to bother me with bad news. Very supportive. Wrong both times.

Word of the Week: Abliterated

Used in a sentence:

"Meanwhile, we've seen the rise of 'abliterated' AI models that are designed to not say no and open weights models that can be used for pentesting and potentially offensive hacks without the guardrails that exist in many off-the-shelf commercial models."

Source: Jason Koebler, 404 Media (October 2026).

Definition: An AI model that's had its ability to refuse taken out on purpose.

In Plain Ammer: A yes man.

See also: [Jim Carrey, Yes Man (2008)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yes_Man_%28film%29)

A Dose of Optimism

Last week I said they're going to cure cancer in our lifetime. I'm not taking it back. This one's about catching it early enough to matter:

[Researchers trained an AI to spot faint changes in the pancreas on CT scans that experienced radiologists missed, on average 475 days before the cancer was diagnosed.](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261024222031.htm)

In scans of people who were later diagnosed, the AI caught 73% of the cancers. The radiologists caught 39%.

Now, before anybody books a scan. This was tested on saved scans, looking backward at people they already knew got sick, not on real patients walking in the door. In one test group of 539 people it correctly cleared just over 81%, which means roughly 1 in 5 got flagged who didn't need to be. The study group wasn't very diverse either, and the researchers themselves say it still has to be tested going forward on real patients.

Still. 475 days.

A Dash of Pessimism

Same article our word of the week came from, by the way.

[Researchers used AI to find serious security holes in PHP, the software behind a big chunk of the web, and after the bug was announced, governments told people to patch fast and places like 4chan, Archive.org and several Wikimedia services went offline for a while to update.](https://www.404media.co/php2shell-black-october-ai-is-changing-everything-about-hacking-no-one-knows-what-happens-next/)

People tried, though. The CEO of Cloudflare said they saw thousands of attempts to exploit it in the 10 hours before the bug was even announced, and millions by the time he posted about it.

Attempts aren't break-ins, and to be fair, 404 is fair about it: they say they haven't seen immediate, widespread exploitation of the bug. Plenty of the AI-written attack code floating around online won't even work. And one security expert, Dan Guido, put it about as plainly as you can: "Knowing how to turn a bug into a shell still takes expertise."

The article even says this is all happening "not because AI has become sentient" but because the software was already full of holes, and now something can go find them a whole lot faster.

Now go back up and reread the word of the week.

The challenge

Go find the oldest thing you can prove you wrote yourself. Not something you had help with, not something you "cleaned up," something you typed start to finish with your own two hands. If it's a Word document, right click it and check the properties, your name might be sitting in the author field like mine was. Give that to your AI before anything else and see if what comes back sounds more like you.

Then tell us in the comments what year it's from and what it was. Mine's from 2017, and it's a sales tip. Somebody out there has a poem from 1998 and I want to see it.
